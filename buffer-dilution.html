What is happening now

  1. Dynamic equilibrium

    Acetic acid gives protons to water and acetate takes them back at the same rate, so the totals hold steady while individual molecules keep changing.

  2. Water is added

    The same moles of each solute now share k× the volume. Water stays at 55.5 M, so it does not appear in Q.

  3. The instant after mixing

    Every solute concentration fell k×. Q has two solutes on top and one below, so Q fell to Ka/k.

  4. Shift to the right

    The forward rate fell k× but the reverse rate fell k²×. Net ionization of CH₃COOH rebuilds H₃O⁺ until the two rates match again.

pH against dilution: the theoretical result

Henderson–Hasselbalch Buffer, exact (ideal) Buffer, with activities (Davies) HCl at the same starting pH

Henderson–Hasselbalch says pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). Dilution divides both by the same factor, so the line is flat.

The exact equilibrium includes the H₃O⁺ that had to be made to refill the solution. While the buffer components are hundreds of times more concentrated than H₃O⁺, that correction is invisible. Once they fall toward about 10⁻⁴ M the correction grows and pH drifts toward 7. The shaded band marks where the gap passes 0.02 pH units.

Activities. In the 0.1 M solution, ions are crowded and acetate's activity coefficient is about 0.78. Dilution lowers ionic strength, γ rises toward 1, and the measured pH creeps up by about 0.1 before the ideal curves take over.

HCl has no reservoir of undissociated acid. Every tenfold dilution removes 90% of its H₃O⁺ for good, until water's own ionization holds it near pH 7.

The mechanism, step by step

  1. Equilibrium is a balance of two rates

    Proton transfer runs both ways all the time. At 0.1 M each, about 8 × 10⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ of acetic acid ionizes and the same amount recombines. Equilibrium is when these match:

    kf[HA] = kr[H₃O⁺][A⁻]  ⇔  Ka = kf/kr

  2. Dilution disturbs Q, not Ka

    Dividing every solute concentration by k changes the reaction quotient by k⁻¹. Two dissolved particles sit on the product side and one on the reactant side. Water's concentration is unchanged.

    Q = (h/k)(a/k)/(c/k) = Ka/k < Ka

  3. The rates answer differently

    Ionization depends on one solute, so it slows k-fold. Recombination needs an H₃O⁺ and an A⁻ to meet, so it slows k²-fold. For a moment, ionization wins. This is Le Chatelier's principle seen from the kinetics.

    vf/vr = Ka/Q = k

  4. A small reservoir draw restores H₃O⁺

    To refill H₃O⁺ to nearly its old value, only x ≈ [H₃O⁺](1 − 1/k) of the acid must ionize. For 0.1 M acetic acid that is under 0.02% of the HA, so the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] is unchanged and so is the pH.

    x ≪ [HA], [A⁻]  ⇒  [A⁻]/[HA] ≈ constant

  5. Where the argument breaks

    When [HA] and [A⁻] are no longer much larger than [H₃O⁺], x stops being negligible and the exact curve leaves the flat line. Buffer capacity also falls k-fold with every dilution, even when pH holds: the diluted buffer absorbs less added acid or base.

    β = 2.303([H₃O⁺] + [OH⁻] + C·Ka[H₃O⁺]/(Ka+[H₃O⁺])²)

Model notes: Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, recombination rate constant kr = 4.5 × 10¹⁰ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ (diffusion controlled), so kf = Kakr ≈ 7.9 × 10⁵ s⁻¹. The real return to equilibrium takes nanoseconds; the animation stretches it to a few seconds and scales the event rate by the same factor. The molecular window is a fixed volume. Each H₃O⁺ dot stands for far fewer ions than each acid or acetate dot, so the H₃O⁺ that is rebuilt is visible while the matching change in HA is not.
