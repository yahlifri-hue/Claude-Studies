Acetic acid / sodium acetate buffer · what dilution does at the molecular scale

Buffer Under Dilution

Ten short animated scenes. Each one zooms in on a part of the story: what is in the solution, how protons move back and forth, what adding water changes, and why the pH stays put while the buffer itself gets weaker. Chapters 3 and 6 are live simulations with over a thousand molecules, where the balance emerges from random collisions.

What to take away

  • Equilibrium is motionProtons pass from acetic acid to water and back again constantly. A steady pH means the two directions are balanced on average. If H₃O⁺ becomes too plentiful it meets acetate more often and is pulled back; if too scarce, the reverse.
  • Water dilutes, it does not reactAdding water spreads every dissolved particle over a larger volume. Acid and acetate thin by the same factor, so their ratio stays the same.
  • Recombination needs a meetingIonizing needs only an acid and a water, and water is everywhere. Recombining needs an H₃O⁺ to find an acetate, and that gets harder as both thin out. The balance tips toward ionization.
  • The reserve refills H₃O⁺A tiny fraction of the acetic acid ionizes and puts the hydronium back. The reserve is so large that its ratio to acetate, and so the pH, barely changes.
  • LimitsOnce the acid and acetate are no longer much more plentiful than H₃O⁺, refilling drains a visible share of the reserve and the pH drifts toward 7.
  • Same pH, less protectionA diluted buffer has the same pH but fewer particles to neutralize added acid or base. Its capacity falls with every dilution.

Not to scale: hydronium is drawn far more common than it is (about 1 per 5,700 acetic acid molecules in a 0.1 M buffer), and real proton transfers happen in billionths of a second. The scenes slow them down and stage them one step at a time so each cause is visible. In the two simulation chapters nothing is scripted: particles wander at random, an acid ionizes with a fixed chance per second, and an H₃O⁺ recombines only when it touches an acetate. The acid there is made much stronger than acetic acid so that H₃O⁺ is visible (about 1 per 90 acid molecules rather than 1 per 5,700).
