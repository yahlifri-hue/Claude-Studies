Acetic acid / sodium acetate buffer · a live molecular simulation

Buffer Under Dilution

Ten chapters built on one live simulation of thousands of molecules. They cover what is in the solution, how protons move back and forth, why a buffer needs both an acid and its conjugate base, what adding water changes, and why the pH stays put while the buffer gets weaker. Each step waits for you, and every step has something to try.

What to take away

  • Equilibrium is motionProtons pass from acetic acid to water and back all the time. A steady pH means the two directions balance on average. If H₃O⁺ becomes too plentiful it meets acetate more often and is pulled back; if too scarce, the reverse.
  • A buffer needs an acid and its conjugate baseAcetic acid alone leaves little acetate to catch extra H₃O⁺. Adding sodium acetate supplies that partner, pushes the equilibrium back (the common-ion effect), and sets the pH by the acetate : acid ratio.
  • Water dilutes, it does not reactAdding water spreads every dissolved particle over a larger volume. Acid and acetate thin by the same factor, so their ratio stays the same.
  • Recombination needs a meetingIonizing needs only water, which is everywhere. Recombining needs an H₃O⁺ to find an acetate, which gets harder as both thin out. So dilution tips the balance toward ionization.
  • The reserve refills H₃O⁺A small share of the acetic acid ionizes and rebuilds the hydronium. The ratio of acid to acetate barely changes, so neither does the pH, as long as the reserve is large.
  • Same pH, less protectionA diluted buffer has the same pH but fewer particles to neutralize added acid or base. Its capacity falls with every dilution.

How the simulation works: particles wander at random. Each acetic acid ionizes with a fixed chance per second (water is always around it). An H₃O⁺ can hand its proton back only to an acetate it is touching, and the same contact rule applies to OH⁻ meeting acetic acid or H₃O⁺. Acetate hydrolysis and water's own ionization are included too. The rates use the real acid constant (pKₐ 4.76) and water constant, so the box behaves like a real acetate buffer of about 1.5 mM. That is dilute enough for H₃O⁺ to be common enough to see. In a typical 0.1 M lab buffer H₃O⁺ is about 1 per 5,700 acid molecules, and every proton transfer takes billionths of a second.
